EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Cambodia’s blue carbon ecosystems, including mangroves, seagrass, and tidal marshes represent
important natural infrastructure that supports climate regulation, coastal protection, fisheries productivity,
biodiversity, local livelihoods, and carbon sequestration. These ecosystems are distributed along
approximately 435 kilometers (km) of coastline across the provinces of Koh Kong, Preah Sihanouk, Kampot,
and Kep. In total, mangroves cover 58,852 hectares, while seagrass accounts for 13,890 hectares. 1

However, these ecosystems continue to face pressures from coastal and unsustainable development,
resulting in the loss of ecosystem services and the release of significant amounts of carbon dioxide into the
atmosphere, contributing to climate change. This study was conducted in response to the rapid degradation
of coastal habitats, evolving global and national climate and biodiversity policies, and the current and
emerging financing opportunities to support coastal conservation and restoration, including carbon
markets.

This study examined Cambodia’s readiness to develop blue carbon initiatives by: (1) assessing institutional
arrangements using a SWOT (strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats) analysis and stakeholder
mapping analysis, which reviewed roles, responsibilities, and power dynamics for stakeholders involved in
marine resource management, (2) analyzing policy and legal frameworks by identifying policy gaps and
alignment, and (3) assessing financing mechanisms by evaluating blue carbon market readiness and
exploring non-carbon market opportunities to advance blue carbon projects.

The findings indicate that Cambodia is at an early stage of blue carbon project development. Most
projects are led by non-governmental organizations (NGOs) through Community Fisheries (CFis) and
Community Protected Areas (CPAs), and focus on conservation, restoration, and community development.
A few blue carbon crediting projects are in the design phase and are seeking approval under the Reducing
Emissions from Deforestation and Forest Degradation (REDD+) mechanism. The current institutional
arrangement for REDD+ focuses on mangroves but provides a strong foundation for expansion into other
blue carbon ecosystems, particularly seagrass. Expanded REDD+ scope could be achieved through
modifications to the existing roles and responsibilities within Cambodia’s institutional framework to enable
oversight of blue carbon habitats.

Cambodia’s policy framework, including the Pentagonal Strategy Phase I, National Strategic Development
Plan (NSDP) 2024–2028, Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) 3.0, Long Terms Strategy for Cabon
Neutrality (LTS4CN), Cambodia Climate Change Strategic Plan (CCCSP) 2024–2033, and the revised
National Biodiversity Strategy and Action Plan (NBSAP) provide enabling conditions for the conservation,
restoration, and climate financing for blue carbon ecosystems, particularly mangroves. Furthermore,
Cambodia has established the greenhouse gas (GHG) inventory, Safeguard Information System (SIS),
Forest Reference Level (FRL), and the National Forest Monitoring System (NFMS) for Measurement,
Reporting, and Verification (MRV). There are opportunities to integrate blue carbon ecosystems into these
REDD+ frameworks.

The findings highlight several institutional gaps and challenges, including limited inter-agency
governance, coastal land tenure rights, enabling policies, and data availability. There is unclear
delineation of roles and responsibilities between the REDD+ Taskforce Secretariat (RTS) and the Carbon

discouraging investment in blue carbon projects. Although the Ministry of Land Management, Urban
Planning and Construction (MLMUPC) plays a crucial role in clarifying land tenure, it is currently not
included in the institutional structure.

In terms of enabling policy, only mangroves are currently included in the mitigation sector of NDC 3.0 and
the National REDD+ Strategy (NRS) 2017–2026. However, seagrass and other marine ecosystems are
included in Cambodia’s adaptation commitments under the NDC and other policies. This suggests that
mangrove ecosystems can access carbon markets under Article 6 of Paris Agreement, while seagrass
ecosystems are more likely to access the voluntary carbon market. Cambodia’s legal framework allows for
public participation and consultation in environmental and natural resources management; however, it
does not yet recognize Free, Prior and Informed Consent (FPIC) as a legally binding standard. Key gaps also
remain in clearly defining carbon rights, and there are no established national mechanisms for grievance
redress (GRM) and benefit sharing mechanism (BSM) specific to carbon credits.

Blue carbon data availability remains a significant challenge, as Cambodia currently lacks a fully integrated,
centralized, and harmonized national blue carbon map. This limitation creates difficulties in estimating
carbon stock in the national marine habitats. Local capacity for carbon stock assessments and
environmental laboratory analysis also remains limited. Although carbon market readiness systems
including GHG inventory, SIS, FRL, as well as NFMS and MRV are in place, blue carbon ecosystems,
particularly seagrass has not yet been incorporated.

Key recommendations
     •   Build on existing REDD+ institutional arrangements, including the RTF, RTS, and REDD+ Technical
         Team by expanding their mandates to cover blue carbon ecosystems, including seagrass.
     •   Integrate blue carbon habitat data, particularly seagrass into the mitigation sector of the future NDC
         updates and the National REDD+ Strategy (NRS) or develop a develop a standalone blue carbon
         strategy to strengthen eligibility for GHG emission reductions and create enabling conditions for
         seagrass in compliance carbon markets.
     •   Integrate provisions on FPIC, carbon rights, and grievance redress mechanisms (GRM) into the legal
         and regulatory framework to ensure alignment with carbon market standards.
     •   Establish a national carbon BSM, including provisions applicable to blue carbon.
     •   Integrate blue carbon habitat data, particularly seagrass, into national GHG inventories, MRV
         systems, SIS, FRL or a parallel blue carbon mechanism to enhance transparency, improve
         emissions accounting, and strengthen readiness for participation in carbon markets and non-
         market financing approaches.
     •   Develop an integrated, centralized, and harmonized national blue carbon map, covering mangrove
         and seagrass distributions across relevant ministries, including Ministry of Environment (MoE),
         Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries (MAFF), Ministry of Land Management and Urban
         Planning and Construction (MLMUPC), and other relevant institutions.

1 INTRODUCTION

1.1 Background

Cambodia’s coastal blue carbon ecosystems—primarily mangroves, seagrass meadows, and tidal
wetlands—represent important natural infrastructure that supports climate regulation and adaptation,
coastal protection, fisheries productivity, biodiversity, and local livelihoods. With a coastline stretching
approximately 435 kilometers, these interconnected blue carbon habitats underpin around 16 percent of
national gross domestic product (GDP) and sustain the livelihoods of coastal communities in Koh Kong,
Preah Sihanouk, Kampot, and Kep provinces.2 In addition to their biodiversity value, these ecosystems
contribute to climate mitigation through carbon sequestration and long-term carbon storage, although,
quantifying their mitigation potential for NDCs or other national reporting depends on reliable ecosystem
mapping, carbon stock data, and integration into national accounting systems. Locally, they are
fundamental to coastal protection, reducing the impacts of erosion and storm surges, and directly
supporting the livelihoods of coastal communities through fisheries, aquaculture, and eco-tourism.

Recent assessments estimate that Cambodia’s mangrove cover spans approximately 55,000 - 65,000
hectares, mainly concentrated in Koh Kong and Preah Sihanouk provinces. 3 Similarly, seagrass meadows,
found along the shallow coasts of Kampot, Kep, Preah Sihanouk, and Koh Kong, cover an estimated 13,890
hectares,4 though updated mapping remains incomplete. These coastal ecosystems have the potential to
sequester carbon and store atmospheric carbon dioxide (CO₂), with storage capacities up to five times
greater than those of terrestrial forests. 5 According to Global Mangrove Watch (GMW), mangrove
ecosystems in Cambodia are estimated to store approximately 76.21 MtCO₂e of organic carbon,
highlighting the strategic mitigation potential of coastal ecosystems for Cambodia’s NDC and National
REDD+ Strategy (NRS) 2017–2026.

1.2 Coastal Ecosystem in Cambodia

Cambodia's coastline spans four provinces, comprising 16 districts and cities, as well as 52 communes
(Sangkats). As of 2022, the coastal population is approximately 1,095,608 people. The area is characterized
by diverse geography, including 75 islands and islets 6, as well as 3,987 hectares of salt fields. 7 This diverse
landscape plays a crucial role in the local economy and ecological balance of the region.

1.3 Key Challenges and Rationale for the Study

Although their ecological importance is increasingly recognized, Cambodia’s mangrove and seagrass
habitats are still threatened by expansion of commercial aquaculture, urbanization, and coastal land
reclamation. 13&14 Unsustainable coastal development, illegal and destructive fishing practices, land-based
pollution, and intensifying impacts of climate change, especially sea level rise, have posted major threats
to coastal and marine habitats.15&16

Existing management and conservation efforts are insufficient to mitigate these threats, especially in
community-managed zones where mandates, enforcement, and funding are limited. This habitat loss
diminishes vital ecosystem services such as carbon storage, coastal defense, and fishery productivity,

Archipelago and Peam Krasop Wildlife Sanctuary have reported continued habitat loss and illegal fishing

be lost by 2035, substantially reducing Cambodia’s coastal resilience and its blue carbon sequestration

resilience against climate impacts and its capacity to contribute to global carbon reduction efforts.

As these coastal ecosystems are degraded, their carbon stores are released as carbon dioxide in a
significant amount to the atmosphere, contributing to climate change. 19 The degradation and loss of these
habitats would result in serious negative impacts on people and the planet, making the expansion of coastal
wetland conservation and restoration efforts more urgent than ever to halt and reverse these declines.

Given the rapid degradation of coastal and marine habitats, recent global and national climate and
biodiversity policy changes, and the expansion of financing pathways for conservation and restoration
including via carbon markets, it is recommended to conduct a coordinated assessment to review existing
national policies, plans, and regulations; review national institutional arrangement for carbon credits; and
explore funding opportunities for conservation and restoration of marine ecosystems through market and
non-market based approaches. 20&21

Conservation International (CI) Cambodia, in partnership with CI’s Blue Climate program and International
Blue Carbon Institute (IBCI), have therefore conducted this study on “Conserving and Restoring Cambodia’s
Coastal Blue Habitats: Policy & Finance Analysis and Stakeholder Mapping.” The study focuses on
identifying key stakeholders involved in blue carbon ecosystem governance, analyzing policies related to
Cambodia’s coastal blue carbon ecosystems, and assessing the enabling conditions for both market and
non-market financing options to support blue carbon conservation and restoration in the country. The
findings are formulated as policy recommendations to help inform the development of a national blue
carbon roadmap in Cambodia.

2.2 Scope and Limitations

The study focuses on blue carbon habitats, following the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change
(IPCC) framework, which serves as the international policy standard for blue carbon ecosystems. The IPCC
provides guidelines for accounting for carbon sequestration and emissions in coastal ecosystems,
specifically mangroves, seagrasses, and tidal marshes. However, because data on tidal marshes in
Cambodia is not available, this study places greater emphasis on mangroves and seagrasses. The
assessment of carbon stocks within all blue carbon habitats is not included in the scope of this study.


3 Methodology
3.1 Conceptual/analytical framework

This study uses a range of complementary analytical frameworks, each designed for specific objectives, to
conduct a systematic and evidence-based analysis of blue carbon management and conservation in
Cambodia. The framework combines stakeholder, policy, and finance perspectives, emphasizing that
successful blue carbon governance relies on the interplay of institutional structures, legal systems, and
sustainable financial mechanisms.

3.1.1   Stakeholder Mapping and Institutional Analysis

The study uses roles and responsibilities, power–interest analysis, and Strengths, Weaknesses,
Opportunities, and Threats (SWOT) analysis to conduct stakeholder mapping and institutional analysis.
These tools help identify and characterize the blue carbon stakeholder ecosystem, which includes national
and subnational government agencies, community organizations, Non-Governmental Organizations,
private sector entities, development partners, and academia.

The analysis specifically examines current institutional structures involved in carbon governance, such as
the CCS, RTF, RTS, the Ad hoc Working Group on Carbon Credit for Partnerships with the Private Sector
(AWGCC), among others. It evaluates each institutional structure’s mandate clarity, coordination
mechanisms, decision-making power, technical expertise, and inter-agency coordination.

Power-interest analysis uses the Swiss Agency for Development and Cooperation (SDC) Learning and
Networking (SDC L&N) tool called the Stakeholder Importance and Influence Matrix. This tool divides
stakeholders into four categories: (1) key stakeholders with high interest, (2) key stakeholders with low
interest, (3) stakeholders with high interest, and (4) stakeholders with low interest. The stakeholders are
defined as follows:

1) Key stakeholder with high interest: These are actors mandated by law to control and manage the coastal
   and marine resources. They possess the necessary resources, knowledge, and expertise in the area.
   They also have a high level of influence over programme outcomes.
2) Key stakeholders with low interest: These are actors mandated by law or regulations to administer the
   coastal area; however, they do not possess sufficient resources, knowledge and expertise in marine
   ecosystem conservation. They have right to veto any program in the coastal area.
3) Stakeholders with high interest: These are actors who are directly affected by the program or stand to
   benefit from it. Their livelihoods highly depend on marine resources; however, they have limited rights
   in decision-making.



6
4) Stakeholders with low interest: These are actors who are indirectly or temporarily involved in the
   program by providing technical assistance for a specific timeframe. They may phase out once the
   project reaches sustainability.

SWOT analysis aims to identify strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats as well as areas for
improvement, particularly to enhance governance effectiveness of the existing carbon crediting
institutional structure.

3.1.2   Policy and Legal Analysis

The study performs a structured review of relevant policies, strategies, laws, and regulations impacting
coastal and marine ecosystems. It examines areas such as environmental protection, community rights,
environmental safeguard and governance (including FPIC, benefit sharing, grievance redress mechanism),
land use and tenure, carbon right, climate change commitment (such as GHG emission reduction target),
as well as strategies and action plans that translate national policy into practice.

The analysis aims to identify gaps, overlaps, and inconsistencies in policies and laws, while evaluating the
level of alignment and coherence among sectors. Special focus is placed on how national policy
frameworks facilitate the integration of blue carbon into Cambodia’s climate and biodiversity goals and
targets, including NDC, NBSAP, National REDD+ Strategy, as well as REDD+ structures and nesting
arrangements. This assessment serves as a foundation for pinpointing priority areas for improving and
developing policies, laws, and regulations to enhance cross-sector collaboration.

3.1.3   Blue Carbon Market and Non-Market Analysis

The study evaluates Cambodia’s readiness to participate in carbon markets within the realm of market-
based finance. It reviews essential institutional and technical prerequisites such as Safeguard Information
Systems (SIS), Forest Reference Levels (FRL), Measurement, Reporting and Verification (MRV), and Benefit
Sharing Mechanisms (BSM). This assessment aims to determine the potential, risks, and governance needs
involved in creating high-quality blue carbon credits.

Meanwhile, the non-market finance analysis examines alternative and supplementary funding options,
including Payments for Ecosystem Services (PES), Environmental and Social Fund (ESF), Ecotourism
income, bonds, and trust funds. These options are evaluated for their suitability within Cambodia’s coastal
and marine environment, especially in regions where carbon market strategies may not yet be practical or
adequate.

For this study, “readiness” is assessed as a staged concept rather than a binary condition. Policy readiness
refers to formal recognition of blue carbon in national strategies and climate commitments. Legal readiness
refers to clarity over carbon rights, tenure, delegated authority, safeguards, FPIC, and benefit sharing.
Institutional readiness refers to the existence of responsible agencies, coordination mechanisms, and
decision-making pathways. Technical readiness refers to MRV systems, GHG inventory integration,
baselines, reference levels, and data quality. Market readiness refers to the ability to generate, authorize,
register, transfer, and sustain high-integrity carbon credits.

3.2 Data Collection Methods

To gather dependable, stakeholder-informed insights, a multi-method approach was employed, involving
tools such as Key Informant Interviews (KIIs), Focus Group Discussions (FGDs), and field observations.
These tools and questionnaires were tailored to align with the analytical framework, which included
stakeholder mapping, policy and legal analysis, and assessment of financing conditions.

3.2.1    Desk Review

The study began with a structured desk review of national and regional instruments relevant to Cambodia’s
coastal and marine governance, blue carbon ecosystems, and financing frameworks. Priority documents
included Cambodia’s Third Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC 3.0), the latest draft of the National
Biodiversity Strategy and Action Plan (NBSAP), the National REDD+ Strategy (2017–2026), the 2023
Environment and Natural Resources Code (ENR Code), the 2025 Fisheries Law, and other relevant laws and
regulations. Collectively, these materials were used to understand the ambitious GHG emission reduction
targets, particularly those related to blue carbon habitats as and to assess their alignment with national
commitments.

The review also drew on scientific literature and project documentation produced by development partners
and implementing organizations (e.g., ADB, FAO, AFD, World Bank, UNDP, CI, F&F, and IUCN) to
consolidate available evidence on ecosystem extent, trends, and threats affecting mangroves, seagrass,
and associated coastal habitats. These sources supported the development of an annotated evidence map
of existing blue carbon initiatives and pilots, with specific attention to enabling conditions, safeguard
approaches, benefit sharing models, and financing mechanisms previously tested in Cambodia and
comparable ASEAN contexts.

3.2.2    Primary data collection

Primary data collection was undertaken in close coordination with Conservation International (CI)
Cambodia, using the stakeholder engagement matrix developed during the inception phase to identify and
prioritize participants. This ensured balanced representation across national and sub-national government

8
institutions, civil society organizations, academia, the private sector, and community-based groups directly
involved in or affected by coastal and marine governance and blue carbon initiatives. The primary data
collection was conducted through KII and FGD.

The study used a comprehensive, multi-sectoral approach to engage stakeholders, involving 51 different
entities across 7 key groups through FGDs and KIIs. Semi-structured KIIs covered 6 stakeholder groups to
assess coastal and blue carbon readiness, involving 6                     ministries, 13 sub-national

Eight FGDs were organized with community members, including coastal residents, CFis, Community

Significant Change (MSC) approach was used to elicit community narratives about key changes, their
drivers, and the significance of the marine and coastal ecosystem for livelihoods, governance, and long-
term sustainability. The study employed a qualitative participatory research methodology. A stakeholder
validation workshop was conducted to validate findings, gather feedback, and build consensus on key
conclusions.

3.2.3   Data Analysis

Data collected through desk review and primary data collection methods were transcribed, documented,
and coded according to the research objectives and conceptual framework. The research team developed
a data matrix in Microsoft Word to systematically organize and synthesize the collected information. Data
were then analyzed using descriptive analysis, guided by the research objectives and research questions.

SWOT analysis was applied to assess the strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats related to the
institutional arrangements and to identify key findings and implications. In addition, Power-Interest Analysis
was conducted to map key stakeholders based on their level of influence and interest, enabling the
identification of stakeholder roles, engagement priorities, and decision-making power.

4.1.1   Blue Carbon Work Landscape and Governance
  A. Coastal Area Management and Governance

Cambodia’s coastal areas are managed through a multi-layered institutional governance structure.
Understanding this structure is essential for effective navigating and implementing blue carbon projects.

The 2023 ENR Code defines the functions of the MoE as including the establishment of protected areas;
boundary demarcation and zoning; mapping of protected areas; enforcement of laws against natural
resource crimes; restoration of degraded zones within protected areas; and support for state land
registration within protected areas (Table 13, Annex 3). Article 367 of the Code explicitly affirms MoE’s
authority to register all protected areas with the Ministry of Land Management, Urban Planning and
Construction (MLMUPC), placing these areas under MoE’s jurisdiction and enhancing coastal land tenure
security. Under Sub-Decree No. 241, however, these functions are assigned as responsibilities of the
General Directorate of Natural Protected Area (GDNPA), in coordination with the General Directorate of
Local Communities (GDLC), particularly in facilitating the establishment and management of CPAs within
protected areas.

However, areas outside the Protected Areas fall under the jurisdiction of the FiA, currently the General
Directorate of Fisheries (GDF), under the MAFF. Article 13 of the 2025 Law on Fisheries classifies the Marine
Fisheries Management Areas into four types: public fishing areas, community fisheries areas, aquaculture
development areas, and fisheries conservation areas. The FiA/GDF is responsible for managing fisheries
resources and aquatic species; granting community fisheries areas; preventing fisheries offenses;
implementing fisheries climate adaptation measures; and developing and executing conservation and
restoration plans or programs for coastal ecosystems such as mangroves, seagrass beds, tidal marshes,
and coral reefs. The identification of these areas shall be regulated by an additional Prakas (Proclamation)
issued by MAFF.

To support coastal management and development, the Royal Government of Cambodia (RGC) established
the National Committee on Coastal Management and Development (NCCMD) in February 2012. The
committee aims to strengthen coordination, management, and development of Cambodia’s coastal areas,
contributing to sustainable and responsible management of coastal ecosystems and the improvement of
local communities’ livelihoods.

The NCCMD is an inter-ministerial body chaired by the Deputy Prime Minister and Minister of Land
Management, Urban Planning and Construction (MLMUPC). The MoE serves as vice-chair, and the Minister
of Tourism (MoT) acts as permanent vice-chair. The committee includes 19 members representing key
government institutions across Cambodia. At the sub-national level, each of the four coastal provinces has
a Provincial Committee for Coastal Management and Development (PCCMD), chaired by the respective
provincial governor. The PCCMD functions as a subordinate body to the NCCMD and its General
Secretariat. It plays a crucial role in monitoring and evaluating development activities, protecting the
environment and natural resources in coastal zones, and coordinating with national and sub-national
institutions as well as development partners to address coastal management and environmental issues.

These stakeholders play key roles in the management and governance of coastal areas (Table 4). The MoE
through GDNPA and GDLC, and MAFF through GDF are mandated to manage specific marine resources

10
(high interest). Meanwhile, NCCMD and PCCMD serve as national and sub-national authorities responsible
for overseeing all development activities in coastal areas, although their mandates do not specifically focus
on marine conservation (low interest). In practice, proposed activities within MNPs and MFMAs require
permits from MoE (GDNPA, GDLC) and MAFF (GDF), as well as consultation with NCCMD and PCCMD.

     B. Marine Habitats Conservation and Restoration

The landscape of marine conservation in Cambodia has undergone a significant transformation from state-
centric management to a Community-Based Natural Resource Management (CBNRM) approach following
the fishery reform of 2000-2001. The first efforts to involve local communities in managing coastal areas
began in the 1990’s to improve local management and food security under the jurisdiction of MoE. 22 These
arrangements laid the foundation for conservation, including boundary definition, rule-setting, and local
enforcement. In the coastal region, the CBNRM concept was formally institutionalized through CFis and
CPAs. The first coastal CFi formally established in Cambodia was Prek Thnoat Community Fishery in
Kampot province, which has served as a landmark for community-based marine conservation since 2009. 23

Building on this foundation, the MFMAs and the MNPs in Cambodia were established in 2016 and 2018
under the jurisdiction of the Department of Fisheries Conservation of GDF of MAFF and the Department of
Coastal Zone and Marine Conservation (DCZMC), currently referred to as the Department of Conservation
of Wetlands, Freshwater, Coasts, and Seas of MoE, respectively. They serve as critical management tools
for supporting sustainable fisheries and protecting and restoring marine ecosystems and habitats, such as
mangroves and seagrass, which are essential for coastal resilience, food security, and biodiversity.
Underpinned by a robust legal framework, these tools were established to structure the engagement of
local communities in managing coastal resources. For instance, the MFMA was designed to be co-managed
by CFi and GDF, 24 while MNPs engage with community protected areas (CPA) in conservation, protection,
and restoration. Community members are primary stakeholders who stand to benefit from project
implementation. Their level of interest is very high; however, their decision-making power is limited (Table
4).

A number of non-governmental organizations (NGOs) are actively engaged in coastal resource management
and marine conservation in Cambodia, including the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN),
Fauna & Flora (F&F), Marine Conservation Cambodia (MCC), Wild Earth Allies (WEA), and the Fisheries
Action Coalition Team (FACT), among others. These organizations play important roles in supporting GDF
and the DCZMC in establishing proposed MFMAs and MNPs, and in strengthening fisheries and coastal
resource management. They also work closely with local communities, providing technical and financial
support, helping protect community resources and livelihoods, facilitating community-led restoration
projects, and ensuring that community perspectives are incorporated into national policies and
institutional frameworks for marine and coastal management. NGOs mainly provide technical support to
government institutions and empower local communities. They do not derive direct benefits from project
development and are expected to phase out once the project reaches sustainability.

While Cambodia’s mangrove, seagrass, and other marine ecosystems are recognized for their climate
mitigation potential due to their capacity for carbon storage and sequestration, the concept of blue
carbon was introduced through science-based studies and policy initiatives. For example, Bastyr, Alex
(2021) conducted an initial blue carbon assessment in Cambodia, 25 focused on an evaluation of mangrove
ecosystems in the Koh Rong Archipelago. The study explored potential carbon financing mechanisms to
support the conservation and restoration of coastal ecosystems. Landesa (2024) assessed the blue carbon
potential of mangroves and seagrasses in two target sites, Koh Kresna Lok Community Fishery and Prek
Kampong Smach MFMA. 26 In 2024, Conservation International (CI) organized the country’s first workshop in
Kampot province to assess the national context for conserving and restoring coastal blue carbon
ecosystems. The workshop identified key opportunities to strengthen conservation and restoration efforts
by shaping a clearer concept of blue carbon development in Cambodia. It also outlined priority
recommendations for advancing blue carbon science, policy development, and financing. Fauna & Flora
(F&F) has been actively engaged in marine ecosystem conservation for many years. Since 2022, F&F has
engaged in seagrass carbon assessment in Chrouy Pros Bay,27 followed by additional monitoring activities
in 2023. In early 2026, F&F is partnering with the International Blue Carbon Institute (IBCI) to carry out a blue
carbon feasibility study that encompasses both mangroves and seagrasses, aiming to broaden
understanding of the carbon storage capacity and composition of these habitats in Cambodia. Recently,
NGOs such as Marine Conservation Cambodia (MCC) have conducted comprehensive benthic mapping to
assess seagrass coverage across four areas in Kep MFMA, outer Kep, Kampot, and Koh Seh.

Scientific evidence has informed national policy development. Cambodia’s third Nationally
Determined Contribution (NDC 3.0) explicitly references blue carbon habitats in both its mitigation
and adaptation measures. This reflects the important role of the Department of Climate Change (DCC)
under the General Directorate of Policy and Strategy (GDPS) within the MoE in facilitating the inter-
ministerial technical working group on climate change and coordinating with relevant NGOs partners to
integrate blue carbon priorities in the updated NDC 3.0. In addition, the DCC is responsible for the
management and development of national GHG inventories and national reports under the UNFCCC.
Without DCC endorsement, provisions related to blue carbon may not be integrated into the climate related
policies documents (Table 4).

UNDP has been actively involved in REDD+ implementation in Cambodia for many years, working to
advance the development of a full nesting framework. However, in recent years, UNDP has increasingly
prioritized supporting blue carbon development. This includes assisting the Department of Biodiversity of
MoE in integrating the development of a national blue carbon roadmap into the 2026 BioFin workplan.

Cambodia’s carbon trading landscape is built on more than a decade of foundational work in REDD+.

registry system, coordinating the REDD+ Technical Teams in conducting technical reviews of proposed
REDD+ projects, and preparing technical recommendations and submitting to RTF for review and approval.

      D. Status of Blue Carbon Development Phases

The Operations Manual for the Implementation of Article 6 divides the carbon crediting project process into
three phases: (1) project pre-implementation, (2) project implementation, and (3) Issuance and
transfer/cancellation.29 The project pre-implementation, or phase one, involves activities such as a
submission of a project idea note to NDA through CCS (or RTS in the case of a forest carbon project), a
request for a Letter of No Objection, and project design and validation. In this phase, CCS or RTS, and NDA
play important roles in reviewing and approving project submissions (request for letter of no objection) and
issuing Letters of Authorization (LoA).

Although a few proposed blue carbon projects have been established in recent years, their overall
development remains at an early stage, with current initiatives primarily focusing on the conservation and
restoration of marine habitats and the improvement of local livelihoods. At this stage, the GDNPA and GDLC
of MoE play important roles in issuing permits for activities within MNPs. Similarly, permits for activities
within MFMAs are issued by the FiA/GDF under MAFF.

Although the NCCMD and PCCMD may not be directly involved in restoration or conservation activities, or
even blue carbon project development, their decisions can significantly influence coastal land-use
outcomes. The NCCMD reviews proposed project sites to ensure sustainable coastal development and
secure land tenure. As shown in Table 4, it is therefore important to keep the NCCMD and PCCMD well
engaged and to address potential concerns at an early stage in order to reduce risks of overlapping land

4.1.2   Institutional Arrangement for Carbon Credit

The institutional arrangement for carbon crediting builds on the existing national REDD+ mechanism, which
includes National Designated Authority (NDA), the General Directorate of Policy and Strategy (GDPS), the
RTF, Technical Teams, the Consultation Group, the Gender Group, and the RTS. In 2024, to complement
this existing structure, the Royal Government of Cambodia (RGC) issued a decision establishing an Ad hoc
Working Group on Carbon Credit for Partnerships with the Private Sector (AWGCC), as well as the CCS.
These new institutional arrangements expand the scope of carbon crediting beyond forestry to include the
agriculture, energy, industry, and waste sectors. The Climate Change Technical Working Group (CCTWG)
appears to have played a role in the new institutional structure by providing support in addressing the
climate change issues in the Kingdom of Cambodia.

In this section, the study aims to review the roles and responsibilities within the existing institutional
structure and provides a discussion how blue carbon ecosystems can be integrated into this structure.

 A. National Designated Authority (NDA)

The National Designated Authority is a national body established through the Article 6 Operations Manual.
The NDA has responsibility for issuing Letter of No Objective for GHG ER projects and issuing Letter of

14
Authorization and Letter of Positive Examination for GHG ER generated from eligible projects in Cambodia
for use under Article 6. Ministry of Environment (MoE) currently serves as the NDA, for which MOE Minister
is the contact person.

 B. General Directorate of Policy and Strategy (GDPS)

The GDPS was transformed from the General Secretariat of National Council for Sustainable Development
(NCSD) in 2021 under Sub-Decree No. 234 on the organization and functioning of MoE. The GDPS plays a
central role in coordinating the development of Cambodia’s national vision, policies, and strategies on
climate change mitigation and adaptation, including those relevant to carbon markets. On behalf of the
MoE, the GDPS represents the Royal Government of Cambodia (RGC) in climate-related treaties,
conventions, and other international agreements that promote environmental sustainability. In this context,
the GDPS, together with support from the DCC, plays an important role in facilitating coordination among
relevant stakeholders to shape national carbon priorities, particularly in the development and updating of
NDC as well as in the management and development of national GHG inventories and National REDD+
Strategy (NRS), which help create an enabling environment for carbon trading.

Given that the current NRS is approaching the end of its term, this presents a timely opportunity to advocate
for the integration of blue carbon ecosystems into the updated NRS, which is scheduled for revision by the
end of 2026.

 C. Climate Change Technical Working Group (CCTWG)

The Climate Change Technical Working Group (CCTWG) is an interministerial working group established in
May 2017 to facilitate and provide technical support to the National Council for Sustainable Development
(NCSD) in addressing climate change issues in the Kingdom of Cambodia. The CCTWG is composed of
technical staff from 19 ministries and agencies and is chaired by the MoE. In relation to carbon credits, the
CCTWG appears to have played a role in the new institutional structure by providing comments and
feedback on proposed carbon projects related to climate change mitigation. CCTWG may perform its
functions upon request from CCS to provide consultation on proposed carbon crediting projects. The
Department of Climate Change under GDPS serves as the secretariat of CCTWG.

 D. Adhoc Working Group on Carbon Credit (AWGCC)

The AWGCC, as a subordinate entity of the Royal Government of Cambodia, was established by Decision
No. 166 of the RGC in August 2024. It is chaired by the Minister of Environment and composes
representatives from 11 ministries and the Cabinet of the Prime Minister. These include the MoE, Council
of Ministers (CoM), Ministry of Economy and Finance (MEF), Council for the Development of Cambodia
(CDC), Ministry of Interior (MoI), MAFF, Ministry of Industry, Science, Technology and Innovation (MISTI),
Ministry of Water Resources and Meteorology (MoWRAM), Ministry of Commerce (MoC), Ministry of Public
Works and Transport (MPWT), Ministry of Mines and Energy (MME), and the Cabinet of the Prime Minister.

The working group is mandated to work in partnership with the private sector to ensure transparency and
compliance with national laws and regulations. It also serves to support the RGC in matters related to
carbon crediting. Nevertheless, the function of the AWGCC will not have any influence on the decision-
making and management of carbon projects. To operate the AWGCC, the Minister of Environment may
assign subordinate body within the MoE to provide administrative and logistical support to the AWGCC for
organizing its meetings.



15
While the Ministry of Land Management, Urban Planning and Construction (MLMUPC) plays a significant
role in ensuring secure land tenure, particularly in coastal provinces, it is currently not represented in the
working group. The presence of the MLMUPC in the working group would be essential to addressing land
tenure security, which is critical for the development and implementation of blue carbon projects.

   E. Carbon Crediting Secretariat (CCS)

To support the administrative responsibilities related to carbon market management, the CCS was
established in 2024 under the MoE. The CCS functions as a supporting body to NDA and administers the full
lifecycle of carbon crediting across five mitigation sectors, including Forestry, Agriculture, Industry, Energy,
and Waste, ranging from the processing of project submissions (request for letter of no objection) through
approval and the issuance of Letters of Authorization (LoA). In addition, the CCS coordinates carbon-related
technical teams and administers the National Registry System. The CCS reports directly to the Minister of
Environment.

The CCS is currently chaired by the Director General of GDLC, who also holds the same roles and
responsibilities within the RTS. The existence of two secretariats with similar functions in forest carbon has
created ambiguity, indicating the need for clearer differentiation of roles and responsibilities between the
CCS and the RTS for certain tasks. However, in practice, proposed non-forest carbon projects are
administered by CCS, while forest carbon projects are undertaken through RTS.

Although the CCS is mandated to coordinate carbon-related technical teams, the composition, scope, and
number of these technical teams are not clearly elaborated or formally identified. In practice, the CCS
requests the CCTWG to review proposed carbon projects and provide technical input before submitting
them to the MoE/NDA for a letter of authorization.

   F. REDD+ Taskforce (RTF)

The RTF is an inter-ministerial body established in 2013 by MAFF, with a mandate focused solely on carbon
credits from the forestry sector. However, in 2016, its functions were transferred to the jurisdiction of the
MoE, leaving all tasks under the responsibility of MoE. The RTF remains responsible for providing overall
management of the REDD+ readiness process, implementing the NRS and reporting the progress to MoE,
and serving as the primary decision-making body within the RGC on matters related to forest carbon credits.

Regarding blue carbon ecosystems, the current RTF mandate is formally responsible for Forestry and Other
Land Use (FOLU) related carbon activities, including mangrove areas, as mangroves are recognized within
Cambodia’s forest-related legal and carbon accounting frameworks. This allows mangrove-related carbon
activities to be linked to REDD+ and FOLU processes. However, other blue carbon ecosystems, particularly
seagrass is not incorporated into the REDD+ framework yet. This ecosystem can be linked to REDD+ in a
complementary manner; however, it requires additional modifications to the core mandate of the RTF. The
functioning of the RTF is supported by the REDD+ Taskforce Secretariat.

   G. REDD+ Taskforce Secretariat (RTS)

The RTS was tasked with supporting the RTF in the execution and implementation of the RTF work plan,
managing the national registry system, coordinating the REDD+ Technical Teams in conducting technical
reviews of proposed REDD+ projects, and preparing technical recommendations and submitting them to
the RTF for review and approval. The RTS is also responsible for mobilizing funding from government
agencies, development partners, NGOs, and other stakeholders to support its day-to-day operations,

       4) MRV Team: Ensure the establishment of a transparent and accurate MRV and Reference Emission
          Levels (REL) system.

such as seagrass are excluded in their terms of reference. To be eligible, revision of the ToR of each
team would be required to incorporate further modifications that encompass all blue carbon
ecosystems.

4.2 Policy and Legal Analysis
This section details Cambodia’s national policies and strategy, sectoral strategies and action plan, and
legal frameworks related to the conservation, restoration, and funding of blue carbon ecosystems, focusing
on mangroves and seagrass. It assesses (i) relevance and scope, (ii) consistency and coherence across
sectors, (iii) enforceability and institutional clarity, and (iv) safeguards and rights, specifically identifying
what is working well, where gaps and overlaps persist, and which reforms would strengthen cross-sector
integration for scalable, high-integrity blue carbon project development and financing. Collectively, these
instruments create an enabling environment where blue carbon could be integrated into Cambodia’s
broader climate mitigation, adaptation, and sustainable development efforts to foster coherent/aligned
action in these important ecosystems.

     4.2.1   National Policy and Strategy

Cambodia’s national policy and strategic landscape provide both the political mandate and the strategic
direction for (a) conserving coastal ecosystems and (b) using these ecosystems to contribute to national
climate and development goals.

The Pentagonal Strategy Phase I is a national development framework introduced by the Royal Government
of Cambodia (RGC) in August 2023 for the Seventh Legislature of the National Assembly. It is designed to
guide Cambodia’s economic and social development toward achieving the long-term Cambodia Vision
2050. The strategy outlines five strategic pentagons; however, the most relevant component is Pentagon
Four, which focuses on resilient, sustainable, and inclusive development, with an emphasis on the
sustainable management of natural resources and ensuring environmental sustainability and readiness to
respond to climate change.

The Pentagonal Strategy Phase I also provides a comprehensive policy framework for formulating the
National Strategic Development Plan (NSDP) 2024–2028, sectoral development plans, and other related
plans, with clearly defined key performance indicators and timeframes. The NSDP 2024–2028, which is the
national policy framework guiding all ministries and institutions in implementing Cambodia’s
socioeconomic development priorities over the period, sets a clear target for restoring 200,000 hectares of
forest annually. It is expected that this restoration would contribute to reducing GHG emissions from 123.2
MtCO₂e to 98.2 MtCO₂e by 2028. This high-level political commitment creates enabling environment for
conservation and management of blue carbon habitats, especially mangrove ecosystems.

To reduce GHG emission, Cambodia put in place the LTS4CN in 2021. It is a national climate policy
document which outlines the country’s long-term vision and pathway to achieve carbon neutrality by 2050.
The strategy identifies the FOLU sector as one of the key pillars of Cambodia’s carbon-neutral pathway, with
the sector expected to be a major carbon sink by 2050. Although the LTS4CN is mostly structured around
forest and land-use mitigation, it provides an important strategic entry point for blue carbon due to the
capacity of mangroves, seagrasses, and other ecosystems to provide long-term carbon sequestration,
climate resilience, biodiversity conservation, and coastal protection.

To balance economic growth with environmental protection, the government put in place the National
Strategic Plan on Green Growth (2013–2030), the long-term strategy that guides the country toward
sustainable, inclusive, and environmentally responsible development. The national plan emphasizes the
management of PES to attract carbon market opportunities and to mobilize funds for the conservation and
restoration of natural resources, including marine ecosystems. These strategic directions provide a clear


21
pathway and enabling conditions for mobilizing financing for conservation and restoration efforts through
PES mechanisms and carbon credits.

To reduce deforestation and forest degradation, while promoting sustainable forest management,
conserving natural resources, and contributing to poverty alleviation, the government developed the
National REDD+ Strategy 2017–2026. The NRS outlines how Cambodia will reduce GHG emissions in the
forestry sector. Mangrove is the only blue carbon ecosystem included in the strategy because it falls within
the national forest definition. However, other blue carbon ecosystems, such as seagrass, were not
integrated into the NRS. As the NRS enters its final phase of implementation, it is expected to undergo an
updating process soon. To ensure that blue carbon becomes feasible and ready for engagement in carbon
markets, it is recommended that the MoE explore options to either incorporate all blue carbon ecosystems
into the updated NRS or develop a standalone blue carbon strategy, in line with the Intergovernmental Panel
on Climate Change (IPCC) requirements for coastal wetlands.

Contribute to the Paris Agreement goal of keeping global temperature rise well below 2°C and pursuing
efforts to limit it to 1.5°C, which Cambodia ratified in February 2017, the country has officially accepted
obligations to reduce GHG emissions and strengthen climate resilience. These obligations are clearly
outlined in Cambodia’s updated Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC), the national climate action
plan that details policy measures for reducing GHG emissions and adapting to the impacts of climate
change. Cambodia launched NDC 3.0 in July 2025, presenting a clear pathway to reduce emissions by up
to 55% by 2035, with major contributions from the Energy, Industry, Waste, Agriculture, and FOLU sectors.

The FOLU sector outlines mitigation measures focused on increasing forest carbon stocks through Assisted
Natural Regeneration (ANR) and other forest restoration approaches. More specifically, it sets a target to
manage and restore 4,080 hectares of mangrove forests. The measure also highlights the development of
restoration guidelines and the sustainable management of blue carbon habitats. The inclusion of mangrove
restoration targets within the mitigation sector would enable the Cambodian government to access carbon
markets under Article 6 of the Paris Agreement and the UNFCCC framework. However, seagrass is currently
not eligible for carbon credit under Article 6, as it is not included in the mitigation sector. It is therefore
recommended that this ecosystem be integrated into the mitigation sector of the next NDC update.

Furthermore, the agriculture sector outlines adaptation measures with clear targets for restoring and
managing blue carbon habitats, including the restoration of 6,120 hectares of mangrove and 4,167 hectares
of seagrass (Table 11, Annex 2). These commitments create enabling conditions for the conservation and
restoration of the coastal resources and could potentially support participation in non-carbon market
approach.

Simultaneously, in support of global efforts to address biodiversity loss, the government is in the final phase
of updating the National Biodiversity Strategy and Action Plan (NBSAP) 3.0 which is a country’s national plan
for protecting, conserving, and sustainably using biodiversity that is developed under the requirements of
the Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD). The final draft incorporates marine and coastal ecosystems
by integrating them into Targets 2, 3, 8, and 19. These targets emphasize restoring degraded areas,
protecting marine and coastal zones, minimizing climate impacts on biodiversity, and strengthening
resilience through Nature-based Solutions (NbS) and Ecosystem-based Solutions (EbS). The draft also
highlights the mobilization of funding from various sources to support the effective implementation of
NBSAP. The targets of NBSAP 3.0 underwent alignment with the Global Biodiversity Framework (GBF).

These policy statements clearly indicate that the government has prioritized green development as a key
pathway to achieving sustainable development within its current political agenda. They also provide

22
enabling conditions for the conservation and restoration of blue carbon ecosystems, while creating
opportunities for financing through both carbon and non-carbon market approaches. However, to fulfill
these high-level political commitments, the government must ensure that these ambitions are translated
into concrete actions.

     4.2.2   Sectoral Strategies and Action Plans

To translate national policy commitments into action, the MoE and the MAFF play key important roles in
developing sectoral strategies and action plans that operationalize these commitments.

In November 2023, the MoE launched the Circular Strategy on Environment (2023–2028) to guide the
country’s environmental governance, climate action, and transition to a green economy, in alignment with
the Pentagonal Strategy Phase I. The Circular Strategy outlines three strategic directions: Clean, Green, and
Sustainable. Among these, the most relevant to blue carbon are Green and Sustainable. Strategy 2 provides
directions for intensifying the national tree planting movement by distributing at least one million saplings
per year, restoring degraded forest areas, and improving Protected Area management. This strategy
includes strengthening governance, updating ecosystem inventories, and enhancing data collection on
forests, wetlands, mangroves, and watersheds across national parks, wildlife sanctuaries, and other
conservation areas. Strategy 3 focuses on ensuring compliance with international conventions and
agreements and aligning with relevant national policies. It also prioritizes strengthening and expanding
cooperation with relevant ministries and local authorities to lead and coordinate efforts related to the green
economy, climate change, and biodiversity conservation.

In May 2025, the MoE rolled out the Cambodia Climate Change Strategic Plan (CCCSP) 2024-2033. The 10-
year roadmap outlines three strategic areas: mitigation, adaptation, and governance to guide Cambodia
toward carbon neutrality and climate resilience. The CCCSP aims to promote GHG emission reduction
through increasing forest cover and half deforestation and strengthening adaptation capacity to climate
change through various measures, including restoring and protecting mangrove and seagrass habitats.
More specifically, the strategy calls for an advanced understanding and implementation of blue carbon
development, which creates enabling conditions for a more comprehensive approach to blue carbon
development, particularly in defining a specific development roadmap.

To enhance climate finance mechanisms, the CCCSP indicates the integration of climate strategies, plan
and policies into fiscal and Public Financial Management (PFM) practices; the strengthening of carbon
market opportunities to generate substantial private investments for climate action; and the expansion of
green financial products such as green bonds and blended financing. More specifically, this strategy calls
for conducting a feasibility study on carbon market opportunities to expand market systems for emission
reduction, including the development of legal and institutional frameworks in accordance with Article 6 of
the Paris Agreement. It also involves exploring ways to mobilize climate finance through carbon trading and
building institutional capacity for the Accredited Entity of the Green Climate Fund.

The CCCSP provides a clear dimension and roadmap for relevant national government agencies to translate
into their sectoral action plans in addressing sectoral climate change, and it creates enabling conditions for
the conservation and restoration of potential blue carbon habitats, including mangroves and seagrass. It
also supports the exploration of financing mechanisms through both carbon and non-carbon markets to
advance blue carbon development in Cambodia.

In alignment with its national strategy and international climate commitments, the MAFF is moving ahead
in developing its Climate Change Priority Action Plan in Agriculture (CCPAP) 2025-2035. CCPAP serves as

23
the implementation roadmap in agriculture sector to build local resilience, reduce the risk of climate
hazards, and their impact on the environment and livelihoods. CCPAP outlines mitigation and adaptation
measures which provide strategic directions to reduce GHG emissions within Agriculture, Forestry, and
other Land Uses (AFOLU) sectors; and to promote climate resilience through sustainable management of
forests. CCPAP set out a priority on expanding forest cover and enhancing carbon sinks. This could be done
through afforestation, reforestation, and forest restoration aiming at reducing deforestation through REDD+
mechanism and other measures. Another priority is placed on the restoration, management, and
conservation of mangroves and seagrass.

The Sectoral Strategies and Action Plans, led by the MoE and MAFF, demonstrate the government’s genuine
effort to translate policy statements into implementation roadmaps and actionable plans addressing
climate change and biodiversity. Importantly, the inclusion of blue carbon habitats in these strategies and
action plans highlights their potential as a key mechanism for mitigating the impacts of climate change,
thereby creating enabling conditions for future blue carbon development.

     4.2.3   Laws and Regulatory Frameworks

Cambodia’s emerging blue carbon sector currently lacks a single, explicit legal instrument to define
and operationalize 'blue carbon.' Instead, various laws and sub-decrees collectively establish
jurisdictional authority, institutional mandates, resource-use classifications, ecosystem
stewardship obligations, and procedural rules for managing mangroves, seagrass, and related
marine areas. These legal frameworks also set the stage for essential enabling elements such as
protecting state public property, environmental and social safeguards, PES and ecosystem service
valuation, protected area zoning, community management regimes, and State land registration.

This section reviews Cambodia’s legal frameworks to examine provisions related to carbon rights, FPIC,
benefit sharing, and grievance redress mechanisms, assessing how these provisions are defined
and what needs to be improved to align with key principles of high-quality carbon and carbon
market approaches.

     A. Cabon Rights

Carbon rights are the legally recognized entitlements to claim, hold, transfer, and benefit from greenhouse
gas emission reductions generated under national authorization and accounting rules. In blue carbon
ecosystems, clarity over carbon rights is essential because it determines who may develop projects,
transact carbon outcomes, assume compliance responsibilities, and receive benefits. Ambiguity over
carbon rights increases transaction risks, weakens investor confidence, complicates benefit sharing, and
may create disputes in coastal areas where land tenure, fisheries management, protected area governance,
and community stewardship overlap.

Cambodia’s legal framework supports State ownership and administration and local stewardship of
forests, land, fisheries, protected areas, and natural resources. The Constitution, the 2023 ENR Code, the
2025 Fisheries Law, the 2020 Law on the Management, Use and Handling of State Property, the 2001 Land
Law, the draft new Land Law, and other subsidiary legislation (Table 13, Annex 3) provide the legal basis for
State administration, local stewardship in protected area management, fisheries governance, and
community access and management rights.




24
In addition, several subsidiary instruments may be relevant to blue carbon governance and carbon-crediting
arrangements, including Sub-decree No. 321 on State Land Registration (2023), Sub-decree No. 171 on
Coastal Area Management (2012), Sub-decree No. 240 on the organization and functioning of MAFF (2025),
Sub-decree No. 241 on the organization and functioning of MoE (2025), and Decision No. 4993 on the
establishment of CCS (2024). Collectively, these instruments provide an increasingly coherent legal and
institutional framework for linking coastal ecosystem conservation with climate mitigation, adaptation,
sustainable development, and carbon market readiness. However, neither the existing legislation nor the
subsidiary instruments explicitly recognize carbon as a distinct legal asset or define the legal basis for
entitlement to carbon benefits. In particular, the legal framework does not clarify whether carbon
entitlement derives from land ownership, resource management authority, community stewardship,
project investment, or the State’s sovereign role in achieving and accounting for emission reductions. As a
result, potential uncertainty remains regarding the allocation of carbon rights and benefits associated with
blue carbon activities.

Even though the legal nature of carbon rights remains unresolved, Cambodia’s Article 6 Operations Manual
provides broader carbon governance architecture, covering project authorization, registry oversight, and
management of emission reductions, rather than serving solely as a mechanism for international transfers.
It recognizes state authority over greenhouse gas emission reductions and allows the Government to confer
ownership of issued GHG emission reduction units to authorized project participants following a Letter of
Authorization and issuance under an approved distribution plan. However, this establishes an authorization
pathway rather than full clarity on underlying entitlements. The Manual does not fully resolve how carbon
rights interact with land tenure, fisheries jurisdiction, protected area management, community
management regimes, or overlapping mandates (ownership) among national and sub-national institutions
in coastal ecosystems. The project proponents should therefore verify tenure, management authority, and
institutional approvals for proposed blue carbon sites before seeking carbon authorization.

For blue carbon, carbon rights should therefore not be treated simply as an extension of land title. Emission
reductions are often generated through the combined actions of State institutions, project developers,
NGOs, sub-national authorities, CFis, CPAs, and local communities. The key legal issue is carbon
entitlement: who may protect, restore, monitor, maintain, transfer, and benefit from carbon outcomes over
time.

     B. Safeguards and Social Governance

This sub-section assesses Cambodia’s safeguard and social governance readiness for blue carbon,
focusing on benefit sharing, FPIC-aligned participation, grievance redress, community rights, and
transparent revenue distribution.

     B.1. Benefit Sharing

Benefit sharing is central to blue carbon governance because it determines how carbon-related value is
allocated among the State, project participants, communities, and other stakeholders contributing to
conservation and restoration outcomes. Cambodia’s natural resource laws provide a useful foundation by
linking management with economic benefits, but they do not yet establish a carbon-specific benefit-sharing
framework.

The Forestry Law (2002) states that local communities within a Community Forest area have the right to
harvest forest products and by-products within the area designated by the agreement (Article 44). The law
also states that any individual who plants a tree on private or state forest land where they have the user

25
rights also has the right to use, sell, and distribute their products (Article 46). Although these provisions do
not specifically mention carbon, they establish a key legal principle that resource management can
produce legitimate economic benefits. This principle provides a legal and policy basis applying benefit-
sharing principles to carbon outcomes from conservation and restoration efforts.

The Environmental and Social Fund (ESF) could provide an institutional channel for directing environmental
and carbon-related revenues toward ecosystem restoration, livelihood support, and local development
benefits. Sub-Decree No. 238 (2016), as amended by Sub-Decree No. 24 (2021), establishes the ESF as a
financing mechanism for environmental protection and sustainable development. The amendment
expanded the ESF’s governance, revenue sources, and eligible uses, including ecosystem restoration,
environmental research, local community development, and carbon credit-related revenues. However,
further implementing guidance appears necessary, particularly on transparent fund management,
allocation rules, and the relationship between ESF revenue flows and project-level community benefits. In
the blue carbon context, the ESF could function as a national redistribution and co-financing platform, but
only if blue-carbon-specific rules clarify eligible beneficiaries, allocation formulas, community benefit
flows, safeguard obligations, and reporting requirements for CFis, CPAs, and other local stewardship
institutions.

The Article 6 Operations Manual 31 establishes that Cambodia holds sovereign rights over GHG emission
reduction (GHG ER) resources within its territory and permits the State to transfer ownership of issued GHG
ER units to authorized project participants once a project secures a Letter of Authorization (LoA) and units
are issued in accordance with the approved distribution plan. The Manual also indicates that the
Government may claim a share of GHG ER units in specific cases, revoke ownership rights for non-
compliance, allocate Government-owned GHG ER revenues to the ESF, and apply administrative or
adjustment fees. These provisions create a basis for authorization, ownership transfer, and public revenue
management, but they do not prescribe detailed project-level benefit-sharing formulas or clarify how
revenues should flow to community-managed coastal ecosystems. Complementary guidance is therefore
needed to align Article 6 authorization, ESF allocation, project-level revenue sharing, and the stewardship
roles of CFis, CPAs, and other local actors in blue carbon projects.

Accordingly, blue carbon benefit-sharing arrangements should be developed through standardized
guidance that links national authorization, ESF management, project-level agreements, and community
stewardship rights. Key benefit-sharing gaps include: (i) no standardized minimum allocation principles for
community-managed coastal ecosystems; (ii) unclear interaction between ESF allocations and direct
project-level revenue sharing; (iii) limited transparency and reporting rules for carbon revenue flows to local
institutions; and (iv) no carbon-specific benefit-sharing principles recognizing communities that contribute
to emission reductions through conservation, restoration, monitoring, and long-term stewardship.

     B.2. Free, Prior, and Informed Consent (FPIC)

FPIC is an important safeguard requirement for environmental and carbon market governance, particularly
where projects may affect Indigenous Peoples, local communities, customary resource use, fisheries
access, mangrove use, restoration areas, patrol obligations, or future carbon revenue flows. Although
Cambodian law increasingly encourages participation, transparency, and stakeholder engagement, it does
not yet establish FPIC as a comprehensive, rights-based consent standard applicable across

potential blue carbon projects often depend on international safeguard frameworks such as VCS/CCB

The 2023 ENR Code provides the strongest legal foundation for participation. Although the Code does not
explicitly state or define FPIC, it incorporates FPIC-aligned elements by requiring project developers to carry
out prior and informed consultation, provide relevant information, and allow affected persons sufficient
time and opportunity to provide input before decisions are made. Article 368 recognizes the rights of
individuals who may be directly or indirectly impacted by environmental and natural resource decisions to
provide informed and timely input through a transparent and accountable process before decisions are
made. Furthermore, during the EIA process, project developers are legally bound to conduct prior and
informed consultation (Article 691) and actively seek agreement from affected stakeholders regarding
mitigation measures (Article 692). These provisions strengthen procedural participation, but they do not
establish consent as a binding legal threshold for project approval.

Article 687 further mandates stakeholder involvement in environmental protection, biodiversity
conservation, sustainable natural resource use, sustainable livelihoods, and environmental impact
assessment processes, with procedures to be outlined by ministerial legal instruments. While these
provisions significantly strengthen procedural participation rights, they do not establish a legally binding
consent requirement, do not define minimum thresholds for what constitutes “informed consent,” and
defer critical operational procedures to future sub-legal instruments. As a result, participation is legally
supported, but consent is not yet established as a binding approval threshold.

The 2009 National Policy on Development of Indigenous Peoples promotes indigenous rights such as legal
recognition of indigenous identity, communal land title, and cultural preservation. However, it does not
explicitly codify FPIC as a mandatory consent standard in environmental or development decision-making
processes.

For blue carbon projects, the distinction between consultation and consent is particularly important. Where
carbon projects affect access to fisheries, mangrove resources, restoration areas, patrol obligations, or
future revenue streams, weak FPIC or FPIC-aligned procedures may create legal uncertainty, reputational
risks, social conflict, and reduced investor confidence. Buyer and investor scrutiny is likely to focus not only
on whether consultations were conducted, but also on whether communities were provided with adequate
information, sufficient time for deliberation, culturally appropriate engagement processes, and a
meaningful opportunity to influence project design and benefit sharing arrangements.

To strengthen governance coherence and market credibility, Cambodia may consider developing a cross-
sector FPIC guideline, regulation, or sub-decree applicable to environmental, land, natural resource, and
carbon market activities, particularly where projects affect Indigenous Peoples and Local Communities
(IPLCs) and customary resource systems. Such an instrument should establish minimum FPIC or FPIC-
aligned requirements, including procedures, documentation standards, timelines, representative
requirements, community decision-making processes, consent thresholds where applicable, and linkages
to benefit-sharing and grievance redress mechanisms.

This would help align domestic law and practice with international carbon safeguard standards, reduce
reputational and fiduciary risks, strengthen community legitimacy and trust, and improve the long-term
sustainability of REDD+ and blue carbon projects.

     B.3. Grievance Redress Mechanisms (GRM)

An effective grievance redress mechanism (GRM) is essential under international REDD+ and voluntary
carbon market standards, serving as a key safeguard and protecting the rights of local and indigenous
communities affected by carbon projects. Cambodia’s legal framework offers multiple avenues for
resolving environmental disputes, but these mechanisms are currently fragmented, and not tailored to
address the procedural, contractual, and carbon-specific disputes arising from REDD+ and blue carbon
market activities.

The 2023 ENR Code introduces a broader framework for resolving environmental and natural resource
disputes. Article 800 provides that dispute resolution mechanisms and procedures apply to claims related
to compensation for environmental and natural resource damage, restoration of environmental harm,
measures to prevent recurrence of pollution or degradation, and prevention of negative environmental
impacts. Articles 801 and 802 further allow any aggrieved party to seek mediation through these
mechanisms before initiating court proceedings, except in cases involving criminal offences.

While these provisions significantly strengthen access to non-judicial remedies, they are framed around
environmental damage and restoration rather than carbon asset governance. They do not explicitly address
disputes related to compensation for environmental and natural resource damage, restoration of
environmental harm, measures to prevent recurrence of pollution or degradation, and prevention of
negative environmental impacts.

Article 803 specifies the institutional bodies and authorities tasked with receiving and managing
environmental petitions and grievances. There is no specific article that defines the carbon-specific
grievance body. The carbon-related disputes may fall into a legal grey area unless clarified through
implementing regulations or ministerial guidance.

In addition to the ENR Code, Ministerial Decision No. 086 on the Establishment of a Joint Working Group to
Receive and Address Grievances Related to Environmental Issues formally assigns responsibility for
receiving environmental grievances (in coordination with relevant MoE departments), investigating facts,
coordinating with local authorities, seeking strategic guidance from senior management, and undertaking
other duties assigned by the Minister. Although the mechanism has not yet been tested in the context of
carbon markets, the Joint Working Group could serve as a foundational institutional platform for addressing
REDD+ and blue carbon-related grievances, provided that its mandate is expanded or clarified to
encompass carbon market-specific issues.

However, neither the ENR Code nor Ministerial Decision No. 086 establishes a dedicated carbon grievance
mechanism. As a result, REDD+ and blue carbon projects currently rely on project-level grievance systems
developed under international carbon standards (e.g., VCS and CCB), rather than on a nationally codified
mechanism. This creates several structural gaps, including the absence of a carbon-specific grievance
body, the lack of procedures for resolving registry-related dispute, limited linkage between grievance
mechanisms and safeguards such as FPIC and benefit-sharing arrangements, and unclear institutional
coordination between the MoE and MAFF in coastal and mangrove areas where environmental, fisheries,
protected area, and carbon governance mandates may overlap. In particular, where mangroves fall within
forest, fisheries, protected area, or coastal management regimes, grievance procedures should clearly
define which institution is responsible for receiving, reviewing, coordinating, and resolving disputes related
to carbon rights, carbon data, project authorization, revenue distribution, and community impacts.
Cambodia should therefore establish a carbon-specific grievance pathway capable of addressing disputes
concerning carbon ownership or entitlement, project authorization, revenue sharing, access restrictions,

28
registry decisions, and benefit distribution. While existing environmental grievance mechanisms provide a
useful foundation, carbon-related disputes require more clearly defined procedures, timelines,
institutional responsibilities, and remedies.

Strengthening grievance redress mechanisms in this manner would significantly reduce the social, legal,
and reputational risks associated with REDD+ and blue carbon projects. It would also reinforce Cambodia’s
compliance with international safeguard standards and enhance confidence among local communities,
investors, and development partners.

    • Mangroves are more advanced for blue carbon development because they are already linked to

    • Cambodia has no single legal instrument defining or regulating blue carbon. Existing laws

    • The Article 6 Operations Manual provides an operational basis for State authority over emission

    • The concept of benefit sharing is embedded in Cambodian’s laws and regulation framework

4.3 Market and Non-Market Financing Approaches

Securing sustainable financing is essential for the long-term protection and restoration of Cambodia's blue
carbon ecosystems. This analysis explores a full range of existing financing approaches related to blue
carbon ecosystems in Cambodia, including carbon market (CMAs) and non-carbon market approaches
(NCMAs), and outlines gaps and recommendations.

     4.3.1    Carbon Market Approaches (CMAs)

Carbon Market Approaches (CMAs) are a key pathway for Cambodia to mobilize climate finance to support
the conservation and restoration of marine ecosystems, as well as to reduce GHG emissions and enhance
carbon removals.

In this section, the study reviews key components including the GHG Inventory, SIS, FRL, MRV, and BSM to
assess the readiness of blue carbon ecosystems for participation in carbon markets.

     A. Bilateral Carbon Market Mechanisms

         1. Japan

Cambodia is engaged in bilateral carbon market mechanisms, especially Japan’s Joint Crediting
Mechanism (JCM). This bilateral framework helps Japan support partner countries in executing greenhouse
gas reduction projects, with the emission reductions shared to meet climate targets for both nations. The
JCM focuses on technology transfer, capacity building, and strong MRV, aligning closely with the new
requirements under Article 6 of the Paris Agreement. 32

According to the latest JCM registry, Cambodia has five JCM projects at different implementation stages,
encompassing renewable energy, energy efficiency, and forest-based mitigation activities. These projects
include solar power facilities, high-efficiency lighting and cooling systems, energy-efficient water
infrastructure, and a REDD+ forest initiative. Most are either registered and issuing credits or undergoing
validation, reflecting Cambodia’s increasing expertise in compliance-level carbon accounting and bilateral
credit schemes. 33

high-integrity Cambodian credits.

      D. Blue Carbon Market Readiness
      Blue Carbon Data Availability

To assess the feasibility of blue carbon projects in Cambodia, it is important to examine the existing data
availability, institutional capacity, carbon storage estimates, and historical land-use patterns. Although
Cambodia has highly valuable coastal ecosystems, moving towards formal blue carbon financing and
policy frameworks necessitates addressing key data and capacity gaps. Across mangroves, seagrasses,
and associated coastal habitats, data gaps are not only technical but also structural, affecting the country’s
ability to establish consistent baselines, emission factors, and long-term monitoring systems that are
compatible with IPCC Wetlands Supplement requirements.

Cambodia's blue carbon data collection relies on specialized departments across various ministries. The
Department of Geographic Information Management (DGIM) of MoE manages GIS databases, monitors
changes in carbon stocks, and develops emission factors. The Department of Biodiversity (DBD) maintains
biological baselines and ecosystem inventories. Meanwhile, the Department of Conservation of Wetlands,
Freshwater, Coasts, and Seas of MoE delineates coastal jurisdictions and conservation zoning maps.
Outside MoE’s protected areas, the Department of Aquatic Resource Management under GDF of MAFF
collects ecological baselines and monitors coastal ecosystem habitats, particularly mangrove, seagrass,
and coral reefs. MLMUPC provides legal spatial data and coastal land tenure boundaries crucial for
securing carbon rights and reducing investor risk. These diverse data sources covering GIS, biodiversity,
coastal, fisheries, and land tenure must be integrated into a unified national monitoring system. However,
datasets are currently developed for sector-specific mandates (protected areas, fisheries management,
land registration), resulting in inconsistent spatial resolution, classification systems, and update cycles
that limit interoperability for carbon accounting purposes.


32
Cambodia currently lacks a fully integrated, centralized, and harmonized national blue carbon map.
Instead, spatial data remains fragmented. Mangrove extent estimates rely primarily on satellite-based
remote sensing products such as Global Mangrove Watch and FAO Forest Resource Assessments, 38 while
seagrass mapping remains incomplete outside the Kep, Kampot, Koh Rong cluster. These data sources
have been extensively used by researchers and NGOs to monitor mangrove extents in Koh Kong, Kampot,
Kep, and Preah Sihanouk. 39 Recently, NGOs such as Marine Conservation Cambodia (MCC) have
conducted comprehensive benthic mapping to assess seagrass coverage. While mangrove spatial
datasets are relatively more developed due to alignment with forest monitoring systems, geospatial data
for seagrasses remain incomplete, often limited to site-specific studies rather than national-scale
inventories. Seagrass distribution maps are currently fragmented across academic studies, NGO projects,
and localized marine conservation programs, with limited temporal consistency needed to detect long-term
ecosystem change.

The government relies heavily on development partners for scientific tasks to quantify carbon stocks, which
are highly valuable. Mangrove and seagrass ecosystems store most of their carbon in deep soils, making
them critical carbon sinks. For mangroves, above-ground biomass estimates are available in selected sites;
however, soil organic carbon measurements remain geographically limited, with insufficient sampling
density to develop nationally representative emission factors. For seagrasses, carbon stock estimates are
even more limited due to methodological complexity associated with underwater sampling, sediment
coring, and habitat delineation. Existing studies provide indicative values but lack sufficient spatial
replication to establish Tier 2 or Tier 3 emission factors under IPCC guidance.

Key gaps across blue carbon ecosystems remain significant and affect Cambodia’s readiness for MRV and
carbon accounting:

        (i) Geospatial gaps: Incomplete national maps of seagrass distributions, inconsistent habitat
            classification across MoE, MAFF, and coastal datasets, limited integration of benthic habitat data
            into national GIS systems, and a lack of standardized coastline change detection datasets.
        (ii) Carbon stock gaps: Limited soil carbon measurements are available for mangroves, and sediment
             carbon data for seagrass ecosystems is very scarce. Additionally, biomass conversion is not
             sufficiently calibrated for local factors.
        (iii) MRV and time-series gaps: The lack of permanent monitoring plots and limited historical datasets
              of Land-use Change for mangroves and seagrass, and capturing conversion hinders the assessment
              of degradation and permanence risk. Additionally, there is weak integration of coastal ecosystem
              data into national GHG inventory workflows, and no standardized community-based MRV protocols
              exist for seagrass.
        (iv) Institutional data gaps: There is limited broader data exchange between MoE and MAFF on the
             spatial datasets, and no unified national blue carbon database exists. Data ownership is
             fragmented among NGOs and research institutions.
        (v) Basic infrastructure gaps: The lack of local environmental analysis laboratories leads to a reliance
            on external laboratories. In addition, the absence of national endorsement or adoption of
            standardized scientific methodologies limits the replicability of blue carbon research and data

       GHG inventory

Cambodia’s GHG inventory is led by MoE and follows IPCC Guidelines, contributing to National
Communications, Biennial Update Reports, and upcoming Biennial Transparency Reports. It focuses
primarily on major emission sectors, such as FOLU, which include emissions and removals related to
forests and land-use changes. GHG Emission Reductions serve as the fundamental currency for accessing
high-integrity voluntary markets and international climate finance under Article 6 of the Paris Agreement.
For carbon markets, alignment with the national GHG inventory is becoming a basic expectation rather than
an option. If Cambodia intends to issue blue carbon credits for international transfer, it must clearly
demonstrate how these emission reductions or removals are accounted for in the national GHG balance.

The absence of coastal ecosystems in the national inventory creates several practical challenges. First, it
introduces accounting uncertainty, as project-level credit volumes cannot be easily reconciled with
national emission figures. Second, it may limit Cambodia’s ability to participate effectively in Article 6
mechanisms, where clear inventory treatment is required to apply corresponding adjustments. Third, it
can reduce confidence among buyers and partners, who increasingly expect consistency between project
claims and national reporting.

Currently, mangroves are the only blue carbon ecosystem recognized in mitigation actions under NDC 3.0,
the GHG Inventory, and other national climate reporting systems. Other blue carbon ecosystems, such as
seagrass is not included, which limits their readiness to access carbon markets under Article 6. However,
voluntary carbon market projects for seagrass restoration or conservation can still be developed under
recognized voluntary standards, even without prior inclusion in the national GHG inventory.

Therefore, including seagrass in future updates of both the NDC and the GHG Inventory is critical. Doing so
would strengthen national accounting, improve policy recognition, reduce carbon market risks, and
enhance eligibility for Article 6 and other regulated carbon market opportunities.

       Safeguard Information System (SIS)

Cambodia’s SIS was established to support the implementation and reporting of Social and Environmental
Safeguards (SES) under the UNFCCC REDD+ framework. The SIS is integrated into Cambodia’s national
climate governance system and operates under the oversight of MoE.

From a blue carbon perspective, the SIS presents both opportunities and challenges. It provides a
recognized national framework that can be extended to coastal ecosystems, aligning with national
safeguard principles and international standards. This is particularly crucial for market-based schemes,
where buyers are increasingly cognizant of social risks. However, the current SIS primarily focuses on
forests and provides limited guidance on fisheries management areas tenure, CFi, Community Protected
Areas (CPA), or marine customary use systems, as indicated in the drafted NBSAP 3.0 and raised by the
stakeholders during data collection.

From an analytical standpoint, there exists a gap in the structural safeguards for blue carbon. While
safeguards are established at the national level, their application to coastal and marine areas remains
incomplete. Without appropriate adjustments, blue carbon projects may rely primarily on voluntary carbon
standard safeguards rather than national frameworks, which could undermine policy consistency and
government oversight. Therefore, it is authoritative to expand SIS to explicitly include coastal tenure,
fisheries access rights, and impacts on marine ecosystems. This will ensure that blue carbon initiatives are
aligned with Cambodia’s overall governance objectives and prevent the emergence of separate,
overlapping systems.

       Forest Reference Level (FRL)

Cambodia has established a national FRL under the UNFCCC, which provides the official benchmark for
measuring emission reductions in the forest sector and underpins access to RBP and carbon crediting
mechanisms.41 This FRL has strengthened national credibility in forest carbon accounting and provides a
clear reference point for both market and non-market climate finance.

Mangroves may be partially included in FRL where they meet Cambodia’s national forest definition and are
recorded in national forest monitoring data. However, available documentation does not indicate that
mangroves are treated as a distinct forest category, and Cambodia’s current FRL does not include soil
organic carbon, which is a major carbon pool in mangrove ecosystems. Seagrass is not a forest ecosystem
and therefore should not be considered directly covered by the FRL. Instead, they would require separate
coastal wetland accounting approaches, as well as dedicated reference levels or baselines aligned with
IPCC guidance.

This gap represents one of the main constraints to scaling blue carbon finance. For market-based
approach, the absence of a national coastal baseline makes it more difficult to apply nesting approaches,
undertake corresponding adjustments, and integrate blue carbon activities into Cambodia’s emerging
Article 6 framework. For non-market approaches, it limits the government’s ability to assess performance
in a consistent way, justify public or donor funding, and compare investments in blue carbon with other
mitigation options across sectors.

        National Forest Monitoring System (NFMS) and Measurement, Reporting, and Verification (MRV)

Cambodia's NFMS is now a functioning national platform supporting forest monitoring, reporting, and
transparency. 42 The NFMS was developed as part of Cambodia’s REDD+ framework and has supported FRL
development, forest monitoring, and climate-related reporting. However, Cambodia has not consistently
published official land-cover and land-cover change maps in recent years, with the last confirmed official
release understood to be from 2018. 43 While internal datasets for later years, including 2020 or 2022, may
have been prepared, these have not yet been clearly confirm that more recent 2020 or 2022 land-cover
datasets have been officially released or used as the basis for current reporting under the 2024 Biennial
Transparency Report (BTR1). 44

The NFMS web portal, land-cover mapping, activity data assessment, and ongoing work on emission factors
and forest-cover change analysis are established components of Cambodia’s climate governance system.
However, this progress has not yet resulted in full MRV integration for blue carbon ecosystems. The NFMS
is still mostly about forests in practice. Mangroves are only partially visible in the system, and seagrass
meadow is not part of its operational scope. Seagrass meadow requires coastal MRV arrangements that
connect fisheries, protected area management, land management, national GHG inventory processes,
registry systems, safeguards, and authorization procedures.

These constraints affect both the carbon market and non-market climate finance. Without integrated
national MRV arrangements, blue carbon project developers often rely on project-level monitoring systems
to meet methodological requirements related to additionality, permanence, and verification. These
systems must then be aligned with national accounting rules, nesting requirements, and registry
procedures, increasing transaction costs and administrative complexity.

Cambodia already possesses many technical components of a functioning monitoring system, but blue
carbon ecosystems span multiple legal and administrative domains, including fisheries, land management,
and subnational governance. As a result, there is not yet a clearly designated institutional arrangement
responsible for coordinating coastal MRV integration within the NFMS framework, comparable to terrestrial
forest monitoring. Integrating blue carbon into Cambodia’s emerging carbon-crediting framework will
therefore require clarification of institutional roles across the RTS, CCS, relevant technical teams, MoE,
MAFF/GDF, and other agencies involved in coastal data and resource governance.

     Benefit Sharing Mechanism (BSM)

Cambodia has accumulated considerable practical experience with benefit sharing through REDD+,
protected areas, and community-based conservation programs. At the implementation level, benefit
sharing has typically taken the form of community funds, livelihood support, local infrastructure
investments, and cost-sharing arrangements between communities, government agencies, and project
partners. These approaches translate conservation outcomes into tangible local benefits and support
community participation in resource management.

In coastal and marine settings, benefit sharing already occurs through existing community-based resource
management, although not always framed as carbon benefit sharing. CFis and Community Protected Areas
(CPAs) benefit from fisheries co-management, ecotourism revenues, donor-supported livelihood
diversification, and incentives tied to mangrove restoration and protection. Evidence from CFis shows these
benefits are meaningful for local communities and contribute to improved governance and compliance with
conservation rules. Nonetheless, these benefits are mostly project-specific, with rules and flows defined
on a case-by-case basis and not yet standardized across sites or integrated into national climate finance
frameworks.

A discussion with Regional Community Forestry Training Center (RECOFTC), which is supporting the review
of REDD+ benefit-sharing procedures in Cambodia, indicates an emerging national framework structured
around fund channels, beneficiary categories, eligible activities, and principles of equity, transparency, and
social inclusion. This work draws on legal review and stakeholder consultations at national and subnational
levels.

Cambodia may consider establishing a more standardized benefit-sharing structure based on three core
elements: fund channels, clearly defined beneficiaries, and eligible activities linked to conservation
performance and community development objectives. For carbon market approaches, the absence of
nationally recognized benefit-sharing arrangements may increase social risk and uncertainty for project
developers and communities. For non-market approaches, benefit sharing is typically embedded in
program design but not consolidated into a unified national framework.

Developing benefit-sharing arrangements for blue carbon may not require new models, but rather better
alignment of existing community benefit practices with national climate and biodiversity policy frameworks.
This approach is consistent with priorities in the draft NBSAP 2026–2030 and the CCCSP 2024–2033, which
emphasize equity, participation, and coordinated implementation. Improving coherence between local
benefit-sharing practices and national climate finance systems can enhance transparency, reduce the risk
of conflict, and support sustainable blue carbon development in Cambodia.

     4.3.2   Non-Carbon Market Approaches (NCMAs)

NCMAs remain essential for financing readiness, restoration, enforcement, community engagement, and
ecosystem protection when immediate carbon crediting is not viable. NCMAs are especially crucial for
seagrass meadows, early-stage mangrove restoration, and community-managed zones, where relying



37
solely on carbon revenues might be insufficient or delayed. Cambodia uses diverse NCMAs, including aid,
funds, ecotourism, PES, philanthropy, and new finance tools like green bonds.

These instruments are considered NCMAs because they direct financial resources toward blue carbon
conservation without creating, certifying, or transferring carbon credits, nor relying on carbon prices to
attract investment. Instead, they achieve climate, biodiversity, and community benefits through tools like
public spending, fiscal policies, community revenue schemes, philanthropy, and sustainable finance,
aligning with Cambodia’s institutional context and funding requirements. This aligns with the Non-Market
Approaches defined in Article 6.8 of the Paris Agreement, which supports climate action approaches that
do not involve generating or transferring mitigation outcomes. There is also potential in aligning public
spending with climate and biodiversity commitments, to free up fiscal space and increase budget
efficiency.

      A. Grants from Multilateral and Bilateral Development Assistance

Official Development Assistance (ODA) remains the largest and most consistent source of biodiversity and
coastal conservation financing in Cambodia. There have been a number of multilateral institutions—
including ADB, World Bank, GEF, EU/EC, UNDP, FAO, and IUCN—alongside bilateral partners such as JICA,
GIZ, France, Sweden, Switzerland, Republic of Korea, Canada, New Zealand, and Norway, finance a wide
range of environmental and coastal initiatives.

There are two funds dedicated to ecosystem conservation, fisheries management, climate adaptation, and
community livelihoods, with a focus on coastal initiatives like the Sustainable Coastal and Marine Fisheries
Project (SCMF), and CamAdapt. Although ODA typically operates through project-specific, time-limited
efforts, it serves a crucial role in testing blue carbon solutions, building institutional capacity, and reducing

      B. Environmental and Social Fund (ESF)

ESF, established under Sub-Decree No. 238 (2016), outlines its structure, management, and fund use to
promote environmental protection, conservation, and biodiversity in Cambodia. In 2021, the Royal
Government of Cambodia substantially enhanced this mechanism with Sub-Decree No. 24, which revised
Sub-Decree No. 238 by increasing the ESF’s revenue sources and permitted uses. Later in 2023, the ENR
Code, articles 770, 771, and 772, provide another legal framework to support ESF. The ESF is now
Cambodia’s leading domestic environmental financing tool, acting as a central platform for gathering
public, private, and market resources to support the country’s environmental, climate, and sustainable
development goals.

ESF funds are legally earmarked for environmental protection, biodiversity conservation, ecosystem
restoration, research, community development, and capacity building, which enables the Fund to directly
support blue carbon–relevant activities such as mangrove conservation, coastal protected area
management, ecosystem restoration, community-based stewardship, and benefit-sharing with coastal
communities. Although the ESF has a strong legal basis and may support activities relevant to protected
area management, mangrove protection, coastal conservation, and environmental restoration, its use as a
dedicated blue carbon financing mechanism is not yet clearly established. At present, there are no blue-
carbon-specific allocation rules, benefit-sharing formulas, eligibility criteria, or reporting procedures that
clearly link ESF resources to community-level coastal carbon outcomes.

With improved prioritization, transparent allocation criteria, and clear benefit-sharing rules, the ESF could
become an important future platform for supporting blue carbon readiness, restoration, safeguards, and
community stewardship. However, before it can function as a blue carbon finance channel, the ESF would
need specific rules on eligible blue carbon activities, eligible beneficiaries, revenue allocation, community
benefit sharing, safeguards, public disclosure, and monitoring of ecological and livelihood outcomes.
Additionally, ESF offers a transparent legal framework to finance the protection and restoration of coastal
blue carbon ecosystems such as mangroves, seagrass, and wetlands. Managed by the MoE, it primarily
draws funds from developer EIA fees, carbon credit revenues from REDD+ projects like Southern
Cardamom, and voluntary private donations—all linked to coastal development pressures.

      C. Payment for Ecosystem Services (PES)

PES is formally recognized as one of the financing solutions under Strategic Objective 4 (“Strengthen
Institutional Capacity and Collaboration”) of Cambodia’s National Protected Area Strategic Management




39
Plan (NPASMP), 2017–2031. 45 The NPASMP identifies PES as a mechanism through which beneficiaries of
ecosystem services such as water users, tourism operators, or carbon markets can compensate those who
manage and protect ecosystems. In principle, PES in Cambodia may take various forms, including fees for
water consumption, biodiversity conservation payments, or carbon-related payments. However,
recognition of PES in policy does not mean that Cambodia already has an operational PES framework for
coastal or blue carbon ecosystems. Extending PES to blue carbon would require substantial additional
groundwork, including identification of ecosystem service buyers and beneficiaries, valuation of coastal
ecosystem services, rules for revenue collection and distribution, monitoring indicators for blue carbon and
biodiversity outcomes, safeguards for affected communities, and grievance mechanisms for disputes over
access restrictions, payment eligibility, and benefit sharing. Existing PES experience remains largely pilot-
based and focused on watershed or biodiversity conservation rather than mangrove, seagrass, or carbon-
specific outcomes.

In practice, PES in Cambodia remains at a pilot and demonstration stage, mainly supported by conservation
NGOs and development partners, and has not yet been scaled into a nationally regulated financing
mechanism for coastal blue carbon. A notable example is the biodiversity PES pilot led by Wildlife
Conservation Society (WCS) at Kulen Promtep Wildlife Sanctuary, where local communities receive direct
payments for conservation efforts. In this scheme, community members are paid USD 15 for each protected
bird nest and USD 4.5 per day for participating in guardian patrol groups.

At the national level, Cambodia's Royal Government, via the National Council for Sustainable Development
(NCSD) has started to create a PES policy framework. Conservation International (CI) and UNDP have
actively supported this effort through technical assistance, research, and policy exchange programs.
Additionally, a study visit conducted by MoE officials to Costa Rica—a country with a well-established
national PES system—further strengthened the government’s interest in tailoring PES mechanisms to
Cambodia’s context.

As part of this process, the MoE selected two pilot sites to test watershed-based PES schemes: (i) Phnom
Kulen National Park (Siem Reap Province), which provides critical water supply to Siem Reap city; and (ii)
Kbal Chhay Multiple Use Area (Preah Sihanouk Province), which supplies water to Sihanoukville. These
pilots aim to establish watershed protection payment schemes, compensating upstream communities for
protecting forested catchments that secure water quality and quantity for downstream urban and economic
users.

While current PES initiatives in Cambodia focus mainly on watershed protection and biodiversity
conservation, some design principles may be adapted to blue carbon ecosystems, but direct transfer would
require substantial additional legal, technical, institutional, and monitoring arrangements, particularly
mangroves, estuaries, and coastal wetlands. These ecosystems deliver comparable ecosystem services—
water quality regulation, fisheries productivity, coastal protection, and climate resilience—that align
closely with PES approaches promoted under UNDP–GEF programming.45F 46 However, PES is not fully
implemented or institutionalized as a national policy or legal framework; its application to blue carbon will
remain project-based and donor-supported, rather than serving as a stable, scalable national financing
mechanism.

       D. Surcharge to Tour Agents for Islands

Various financial options have been identified for the Koh Rong Archipelago Marine Fisheries Management
Area (now known as Koh Rong Marine Park), such as voluntary donations, additional fees for diving and
boating, concession fees, and souvenirs. 47

The administration of Preah Sihanouk province is in charge of collecting a fee of 8.000 Riel (US$2) for each
tourist using a tour agent boat. Preah Sihanouk provincial authority keeps these revenues for the coastal
area development, maintaining security, improving hygiene, and protecting the environment in the province.
These charges apply to all tourist boats transferring visitors to the province's islands, especially the Koh
Rong Marine Park and surrounding islands.

Until 2017, the provincial authority formalized the fee collection by requesting approval from the Ministry of
Economy and Finance (MEF). The MEF responded by issuing Letter No. 6864, officially authorizing the
provincial authority to collect the fees from both national and international tourists via boat operators and
utilize the revenue for coastal development and environmental protection. The islands have become a
highly popular coastal tourism destination, with an estimated 1 million tourists visiting in 2019.48

       E. Levy Airport Taxes to Offset GHG Emissions

Cambodia has implemented a small aviation environmental surcharge, currently US$1 per airline ticket, as
a domestic fiscal tool to promote environmental protection and climate initiatives. Funds collected from
this surcharge go directly to the Environmental and Social Fund (ESF). While the total revenue from the
surcharge is modest, its significance lies in setting a precedent for targeted environmental levies that are
both politically acceptable and practical to administer within Cambodia’s public finance system.

This mechanism aligns with and complements tourism-related fees and surcharges in coastal and island
areas, such as the boat fee in Preah Sihanouk Province for Koh Rong Marine Park access. These measures
demonstrate a unified approach where sector-specific charges from environmentally sensitive or high-
emission activities are pooled and reinvested in conservation and coastal management. Building on these
practices, similar levy-based systems could be expanded systematically across tourism, maritime
transport, and coastal development sectors, with ESF serving as the main platform for collecting and
allocating funds to support blue carbon management and marine ecosystem protection.

been formally established nationwide. 49 Revenue streams typically include visitor entrance fees and

Within coastal areas, CBET functions as a non-carbon market financing mechanism that contributes to the

formula: 20 percent is allocated to the CFi management functions; 20 percent is distributed to participating
members as incentives; and 20 percent covers operational costs, including water, electricity, and hygiene
services. The remaining 40 percent is allocated to community priorities, including CBET development,
conservation activities, enterprise development, and gender-related initiatives. 50

Evidence from community consultations indicates that this structured benefit-sharing arrangement
strengthens accountability, improves perceptions of fairness, and supports compliance with conservation
rules. By linking livelihood incentives with conservation, the Kampong Samaki model illustrates how locally
governed benefit-sharing mechanisms can support sustainable resource management. While CBET
operates primarily as a non-carbon market mechanism, it also contributes to institutional readiness for
future carbon finance engagement by strengthening governance capacity, transparency, and community
participation.

        G. Corporate Voluntary Donations for Conservation

In Cambodia, two hotels located within the Kep National Park, the foreigner-owned Knai Bang Chatt Hotel
and the Khmer-owned Veranda Natural Resort, have a contract with MoE to pay US$1 per room per night to
the ministry. The funds collected from this voluntary contribution are specifically allocated for the
management of Kep National Park. The hotel owners agreed to this financial contribution because they
recognize that the park's natural landscape is crucial for attracting tourists and sustaining their business
operations. This arrangement is an excellent, scalable model for other businesses that rely directly on the
preservation of nature for their commercial success.

        H. Mobilization of Private Foundation Donation

Biodiversity conservation can mobilize resources from private donations and philanthropies through
different fund-raising strategies and marketing campaigns by individual organizations. Fund can be raised
from private citizens including memberships, fundraising events, crowdfunding, etc. Currently, there are
many NGOs working in Cambodia in the fields of biodiversity conservation and biodiversity-related
livelihood development. Some of their funding sources are from private foundations, particularly
philanthropists such The Prince Albert II of Monaco Foundation for the conservation of Koh Rong Marine
Park through Fauna and Flora and Fondation Ensemble who fund conservation work of Marine Conservation

     I.   Blended Financing for Conservation

Blended finance has become an increasingly vital mechanism for mobilizing long-term and scalable
investments in blue carbon and coastal ecosystem conservation, especially in developing countries where
market risks, unclear land rights, and limited revenue certainly hinder private sector involvement. Blended
financing for blue carbon involves strategically combining public finance, concessional capital, and
philanthropic funding to reduce investment risks and attract private capital for the conservation,
restoration, and sustainable management of mangroves, seagrass, and related coastal livelihoods.51&52

Blue carbon ecosystems provide important global public goods such as climate change mitigation, coastal
protection, biodiversity conservation, and food security, yet many of these benefits are not fully priced in
markets. As a result, pure commercial funding is often insufficient or unsuitable, especially in early project
stages or in areas with high social and ecological sensitivity. Blended finance fills this gap by enabling
concessional and grant-based capital to absorb initial risks, support enabling conditions (like governance,
monitoring, and tenure clarification), and enhance the bankability of coastal nature-based solutions over
time (UNEP, 2023). 53

In Cambodia, the Sustainable Landscapes and Ecotourism Project (CSLEP) demonstrates how blended
finance can promote conservation efforts directly benefiting coastal and blue carbon ecosystems. Although
primarily landscape-focused, CSLEP includes coastal provinces where mangroves and nearshore
ecosystems contribute to ecotourism, fisheries, and climate resilience. The project combines concessional
financing from the World Bank's International Development Association (IDA), with grant funding from the
Global Environment Facility (GEF) 54 enabling sustained investment in protected area management,
community ecotourism, and sustainable value chains linked to ecosystem conservation.

From a blue carbon view, blended finance can support essential enabling conditions, including coastal land
tenure clarification, marine spatial planning, MRV development, and safeguards implementation for both
carbon and non-carbon funding. It can also link climate finance with blue economy activities such as
sustainable fisheries, ecotourism, and mangrove value chains, diversifying revenue beyond carbon
markets. By reducing early-stage risks, blended finance can improve the viability of future carbon projects
while maintaining public oversight and environmental safeguards.

Overall, blended financing represents a practical approach for supporting blue carbon conservation in
Cambodia’s coastal zones. As national policy frameworks increasingly recognize mangroves and seagrass
ecosystems as critical natural assets, blended finance can help connect public climate finance, private
sector investment, and community-level governance. When supported by clear governance, transparent
benefit-sharing, and credible safeguards, blended finance can contribute to long-term ecosystem
conservation while supporting inclusive and climate-resilient coastal development.

5 Conclusion and Recommendations
5.1 Conclusion

This study examined Cambodia’s readiness to develop blue carbon initiatives by: (1) assessing institutional
arrangements, mapping stakeholders involved in marine resource management; (2) analyzing policy legal
frameworks, and (3) assessing financing mechanisms to advance blue carbon project.

The findings indicate that Cambodia is at an early stage of blue carbon development. The majority of
projects are NGO-run initiatives implemented through CFIs and community protected areas (CPAs), with a
primary focus on marine resource conservation, restoration, and community development. However, a few
projects are currently in the design phase and aim to obtain permits from the Ministry of Environment for
blue carbon projects under the existing REDD+ mechanism.

Cambodia’s policy framework increasingly recognizes the importance of blue carbon, as reflected in key
national strategies such as the Pentagonal Strategy Phase I, NSDP 2024–2028, NDC 3.0, LTS4CN, CCCSP
2024–2033, the NBSAP 3.0, and various sectoral strategies. These frameworks collectively create strong
enabling conditions for conservation, restoration, and climate finance.

In the context of REDD+, Cambodia has established many readiness elements, including GHG inventory,
SIS, FRL, and NFMS/MRV. Furthermore, Cambodia has been piloting a national nesting framework, which
aims to update regulation, guidelines, and handbook to establish many core readiness elements. These
efforts provide a strong foundation for integrating blue carbon into the existing systems.

However, the findings also indicate key gaps that require strong attention from responsible government
agencies to enable blue carbon credits to be brought online. For the institutional arrangement, there is a
lack of clarity regarding the roles and responsibilities between the RTS and the CCS, both of which
undertake somewhat overlapping activities. Some members of the RTS have moved to other senior
positions within the MoE, leaving vacancies and raising concerns about the secretariat’s ability to function
effectively. The findings further suggest that only mangrove ecosystems fall under the jurisdiction of the RTF
and RTS, while other blue carbon ecosystems, such as seagrass remain outside their mandates.

The REDD+ Consultation Group and Gender Group demonstrate inclusiveness in REDD+ by ensuring social
and environmental safeguards and gender mainstreaming; however, these groups are optional platforms.
They are operated upon request and are subject to the availability of funds. The findings also indicate that
coastal land tenure is a significant challenge in coastal provinces, creating uncertainty and reducing
investor confidence in the development of carbon projects. The Ministry of Land Management, Urban
Planning and Construction (MLMUPC) plays a critical role in clarifying land tenure security, particularly in
coastal areas; however, it is not currently represented in the institutional structure.

In the mitigation sector of NDC 3.0, only mangrove is specified, while seagrass is not included, meaning that
currently only mangrove is eligible for the compliance carbon market. Furthermore, the National REDD+
Strategy 2017–2026 does not address seagrass. Given Cambodia's current direction toward integrated
carbon governance and nesting, it may be useful to consider whether integration into the next update of the
NRS, as well as into carbon crediting and climate governance systems, should be considered alongside or
potentially preferred to the development of a standalone blue carbon strategy.




45
Cambodia’s legal framework supports public participation and prior consultation; however, it does not
establish FPIC as a binding standard. In addition, provisions on carbon rights remain unclear, and national
grievance redress mechanisms (GRM) and BSM specifically for carbon credits have not yet been developed.

Blue carbon data availability remains a significant challenge, as Cambodia currently lacks a fully integrated,
centralized, and harmonized national blue carbon map. This limitation creates difficulties in estimating
carbon stock in the national marine habitats, and local capacity for carbon stock assessments and
environmental laboratory analysis also remains limited. Although carbon market readiness systems
including GHG inventory, SIS, FRL, and NFMS/MRV are in place, blue carbon ecosystems, particularly
seagrasses have not yet been incorporated into these systems.

5.2 Recommendations

Based on the analysis, discussions, and conclusions, the study provides the following suggestions and
recommendations:

     5.2.1   Strengthening Institutional Arrangement

     Ministry of Environment

     •   Clarify and distinguish the roles and responsibilities of the REDD+ Taskforce Secretariat (RTS) and
         the Carbon Credit Secretariat (CCS) in managing the Registry System and other functions to avoid
         overlapping, given that both secretariats operate within the same forest carbon framework.
     •   Replenish the membership of the RTS to ensure it functions fully and effectively, as its current
         composition and operational capacity may require updating due to member transitions to other
         positions. Updating or confirming membership will strengthen the Secretariat’s ability to carry out
         its technical and coordination functions.
     •   Revise the Terms of Reference (ToR) to expand the roles and responsibilities of the RTF, RTS, and
         Technical Teams to include oversight and coordination of blue carbon ecosystems, particularly
         seagrass, ensuring alignment with an emerging national carbon governance system as well as
         broader climate and ecosystem management frameworks beyond the traditional forest-focused
         REDD+ scope.
     •   Strengthen stakeholder engagement by safeguarding implementation and gender mainstreaming
         mechanisms, including consideration of reactivating existing REDD+ Consultation Group and
         Gender Group where appropriate.
     •   Develop a phased pathway for integrating blue carbon ecosystems into Cambodia's emerging
         nesting and carbon-crediting framework, including project registration, safeguards reporting, MRV
         requirements, registry integration, accounting procedures and future Article 6 participation.

     Adhoc Working Group on Carbon Credit (AWGCC)

     •   Include the Ministry of Land Management, Urban Planning, and Construction (MLMUPC) in the
         AWGCC to strengthen land tenure clarity and security, particularly in coastal areas. Their
         participation is essential to resolving tenure-related challenges and enabling the effective
         development and implementation of blue carbon projects.

     5.2.2   Developing Policy and Legal Framework

     Ministry of Environment

     •   Integrate blue carbon habitat data, particularly seagrass into the mitigation sector of the future NDC
         updates and the National REDD+ Strategy (NRS) or develop parallel mechanism (standalone

         alignment with the Paris Agreement under the UNFCCC framework.
     •   Establish a cross-sector FPIC sub-decree and guideline that is applicable to environmental, land,

     •   Establish a national carbon BSM, including provisions applicable to blue carbon. The BSM should

         community stewardship rights, to ensure transparent and equitable distribution of benefits.

     Ministry of Agriculture Forestry and Fishery

     •   Accelerate the registration of MFMAs as public state land to strengthen coastal land tenure security,

     5.2.3   Strengthening Access to Carbon Market and Non-market financing approaches

     Ministry of Environment

     •   Coordinate through existing interministerial mechanisms or establish a new interministerial working

         mangrove and seagrass distributions across relevant ministries, including MoE, MAFF, MLMUPC,
         and other relevant institutions.
     •   Conduct national carbon stock assessments and strengthen the capacity of responsible agencies
         by providing technical training on the application of standardized scientific methodologies,
         research and data collection, and laboratory capacity for soil carbon analysis.
     •   Establish local environmental analysis laboratories to support timely and cost-effective carbon
         stock analysis, while building national technical capacity and ensuring compliance with
         international quality standards to reduce reliance on external laboratories.
     •   Improve access to non-carbon market financing mechanisms to support the conservation and
         restoration of blue carbon ecosystems, particularly by leveraging instruments such as PES and ESF
         to ensure sustainable financing beyond carbon markets.
     •   Integrate blue carbon habitat data, particularly seagrass, into the national GHG inventory and MRV
         frameworks to enhance transparency, improve emissions accounting, and support readiness for
         participation in carbon markets under Article 6. This should be supported by methodologies and
         monitoring systems appropriate for blue carbon ecosystems, complementing forest-focused
